A Model For Asset Prices

A variable whose value changes randomly through time is said to follow a stochastic process.

For exampple, the most well known assumption made about asset price behavior , which was made by Black and Scholes in 1973, is geometric Brownian Motion(GBM). A non-dividend paying asset, S, following GBM is governed by the Stochastic differential equation (SDE):

\[ dS = \mu S dt + \sigma S dz \tag{1.1} \]

where \(\mu\) and \(\sigma\) are known constants, and \(dS\) represents the change in the level of the asset price over a small interval of time \(dt\). Dividing through \(S\) we get :

\[ \frac{dS}{S} = \mu dt + \sigma dz \tag{1.2} \]

From the equation (1.2), we see that the percentage change or return in the asset price , \(dS/S\), has two components.

  • The first component says that during the small interval of time\(dt\), the average return on the asset is \(\mu dt\), this is deterministic and the parameter \(\mu\) is known as the drift.
  • Added to this drift term is a random component mae up of a change, \(dz\), in a random variable \(z\), and a parameter \(\sigma\) which is generally referred to as the volatility of the asset.

The random variable \(z\) , or equivalently the change \(dz\), is called a Wiener or Brownian motion process. Two key properties defined a Wiener process, firstly \(dz\) is normally distributed with mean zero and variance \(dt\), and secondly,the values of \(dz\) over two different , non-overlapping increments of time are independent.

Equation (1.1) or (1.2) are examples of Ito process, as the drift and volatility only depend on the current value of the variable (the asset price) and time.

In general the stochastic differnt equation for a variable \(S\) following an Ito’s process is :

\[ dS = \mu(S,t) dt + \sigma(S,t) dz \tag{1.3} \]

where the functions \(\mu(S,t)\) and \(\sigma(S,t)\) are general functions for drift and volatility. Many models for the behaviour of asset prices assume that the future evolution of asset depends only on its present value and not on the path taken to reach that level. A stochastic process possessing this property is known as Markovian.

From the assumption about the behavior of the asset price, we can deduce the stochastic process followed by any derivative security whose pay-off is dependent on that asset.

The Black-Scholes Partial Differential Equation

The stochastic differential equation for the asset price \(S\) is the starting point for any derivative model. A derivative whose value \(C\) depends on asset price \(S\) at time \(t\) can be thought of as a function \(C(t,S)\) of the random asset price and time. In order to be able to work with random variables and function of random variables, we need to use Stochastic calculus. One of the most important results in Stochastic calculus is Ito’s Lemma which tells us how to compute the stochastic processes followed by function of stochastic variables.

Ito’s lemma states that the process followed by \(C(t,S)\), where \(S\) obeys equation (1.3) is given by

\[ dC = \bigg(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \mu(t,S) + \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2 (t,S)\bigg) dt + \bigg(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \sigma(t,S)\bigg)dz \tag{1.4} \]

For example if \(S\) follows the process given by equation (1.1), then \(C\) evolves according to the process:

\[ dC = \bigg(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \mu S + \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2 S^2\bigg) dt + \bigg(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \sigma S\bigg)dz \tag{1.5} \]

In the same way as for the equation (1.1), the process for \(C\) has two components which can be interpreted as drift and volatility. The drift term is deterministic, involving the drift and volatility parameters of the asset prices and the partial differential of \(C\) with respect to \(S\) and \(t\).

The most important point to notice from equation (1.5) is that the source of randomness, dz , is exactly the same as that in the asset price process.

Because both the process for the asset and the process for the derivative have the same source of uncertainity, it is possible to combine the two securities in a portfolio in such a way as to eliminate that uncertainty. Consider a portfolio \(P\) consisting of a short position in the option and a long position of \(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S}\) units of the underlying asset. The value of the portfolio is therefore,

\[ P = -C + \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} S \tag{1.6} \]

The value of the portfolio changes as the value of the asset, and hence the option value, changes. The change in the value of the portfolio over the period of time dt is described by

\[ dP = -dC + \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} dS \tag{1.7} \]

Substituting for \(dC\) and \(dS\) in equation (1.7) and using equations (1.1) and (1.5) we obtain

\[ dP = - \bigg(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \mu S + \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2 S^2\bigg) dt - \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \sigma S dz + \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} ( \mu S dt + \sigma S dz) \tag{1.8} \]

Collecting terms involving \(dt\) and \(dz\) together we get

\[ dP = - \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \mu S dt - \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} dt - \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2 S^2 dt + \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \mu S dt - \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \sigma S dz + \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \sigma S dz \tag{1.9} \]

Finally, simplifying gives

\[ dP = - \bigg( \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} dt + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2} \sigma^2 S^2 \bigg) dt \tag{1.10} \]

The change in the value of the portfolio during \(dt\) is independent of the source of randomness \(dz\). The portfolio is therefore riskless and in equilibrium it must earn the riskless rate of interrest \(r\) which we can write as :

\[ \frac{dP}{P} = \frac{- \bigg( \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\bigg) dt }{-C + \frac{\partial C}{\partial S}S } \approx r dt \tag{1.11} \]

Rearranging this formula leads to the well-known Black-Scholes partial differential equation :

\[ - \bigg( \frac{\partial C} {\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\bigg) dt = r \bigg(-C + \frac{\partial C} {\partial S} S \bigg) dt \]

\[ \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + rS \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2} -rC = 0 \tag{1.12} \]

The above equation holds for any derivate whose pay-off depends only on \(S\) and \(t\). For example it holds for standard European call and put options, as well as for American options, lookback options and barrier options.

If the maturity date of the option is given by \(T\) and the strike price is given by \(K\) then these boundary conditions can be expressed as

\[ C_T = max(0,S_T - K) \]

for a call option

\[ C_T = max(0,K -S_T) \]

for a put option.

Apart from the fundamental assumption describing the asset price movements, other assumptions that we implicitly made when deriving equation (1.12) are that the short selling of securities with full use of the proceeds if permitted, there are no transaction costs, all securities are infinitely divisible, no dividents are paid during the life of the derivative security, the riskless rate of interest is constent and the same for all maturities, and that security trading is continuous.

Perhaps surprisingly, the expected return on the asset \((\mu)\) does not appear in the Black-Scholes partial differential equation. Since the expected return on the asset does not appear in the Black-Scholes partial differential equation the value of the derivative must be independent of investor risk preferences.

Therefore, one can assume any risk preferences in the valuation of the derivative or put another way, one can assume the drift of asset price be anything . Assuming risk neutrality , i.e investors are risk neutral, then all assets earn the riskless rate of interest, and equation (1.1) can be rewritten as

\[ dS = rS dt + \sigma S dz \tag{1.13} \]

If the asset pays out a continuous dividend yield at a rate \(\delta\) per annum, then the drift rate is reduced by this payout and the process becomes.

\[ dS = (r-\delta)S dt + \sigma S dz \tag{1.14} \]

The implication of this risk-neutral pricing is that the present value of any future random cash flow ( eg. the pay-off to an option) is given by the expected value of the random future value discounted at the riskless rate. This leads to the so-called expectations approach of option valuation , e.g for a European call option :

\[ C = E_t^* [ e^{- \int_t^T r_u du} max(0,S_T - K)] \tag{1.15} \]

where \(E_t^*[.]\) is the expectation at time \(t\), the valuation date, under the risk-neutral probabilities. For constant interest rates , the above equation can be written as

\[ C = e^{-r(T-t)} E_t^* [ max(0,S_T-K)] \tag{\hspace{0.5cm}1.16} \]

## The Black-Scholes Partial Differential Equation

The stochastic differential equation for the asset price $S$ is the starting point for any derivative model. A derivative whose value $C$ depends on asset price $S$ at time $t$ can be thought of as a function $C(t,S)$ of the random asset price and time.  In order to be able to work with random variables and function of random variables, we need to use Stochastic calculus. One of the most important results in Stochastic calculus is **Ito's Lemma** which tells us how to compute the stochastic processes followed by function of stochastic variables. 

Ito's lemma states that the process followed by $C(t,S)$, where $S$ obeys equation (1.3) is given by 

$$
dC = \bigg(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \mu(t,S) + \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2 (t,S)\bigg) dt + \bigg(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \sigma(t,S)\bigg)dz \tag{1.4}
$$

For example if $S$ follows the process  given by equation (1.1), then $C$ evolves according to the process: 

$$
dC = \bigg(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \mu S + \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2 S^2\bigg) dt + \bigg(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \sigma S\bigg)dz  \tag{1.5}
$$

In the same way as for the equation (1.1), the process for $C$ has two components which can be interpreted as **drift** and **volatility**. The drift term is deterministic, involving the drift and volatility parameters of the asset prices and the partial differential of $C$ with respect to $S$ and $t$.

The most important point to notice from equation (1.5) is that the source of randomness, dz , is exactly the same as that in the asset price process. 

Because both the process for the asset and the process for the derivative have the same source of uncertainity, it is possible to combine the two securities in a portfolio in such a way as to eliminate that uncertainty. Consider a portfolio $P$ consisting of a short position in the option and a long position of $\frac{\partial C}{\partial S}$ units of the underlying asset.  The value of the portfolio is therefore, 

$$
P = -C + \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} S \tag{1.6}
$$

The value of the portfolio changes as the value of the asset, and hence the option value, changes. The change in the value of the portfolio over the period of time dt is described by  

$$
dP = -dC + \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} dS \tag{1.7}
$$

Substituting for $dC$ and $dS$ in equation (1.7) and using equations (1.1) and (1.5) we obtain 

$$
dP = - \bigg(\frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \mu S + \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2 S^2\bigg) dt - \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \sigma S dz +  \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} ( \mu S dt + \sigma S dz) \tag{1.8}
$$

Collecting terms involving $dt$ and $dz$ together we get 

$$
dP = - \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \mu S dt - \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} dt - \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\sigma^2 S^2 dt + \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \mu S dt - \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \sigma S dz + \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} \sigma S dz \tag{1.9}
$$

Finally, simplifying gives 

$$
dP = - \bigg( \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} dt + \frac{1}{2} \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2} \sigma^2 S^2  \bigg) dt \tag{1.10}
$$

The change in the value of the portfolio during $dt$ is independent of the source of randomness $dz$. The portfolio is therefore riskless and in equilibrium it must earn the riskless rate of interrest $r$ which we can write as :

$$
\frac{dP}{P} = \frac{- \bigg( \frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + \frac{1}{2}\sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\bigg) dt }{-C + \frac{\partial C}{\partial S}S } \approx r dt \tag{1.11} 
$$

Rearranging this formula leads to the well-known **Black-Scholes** partial differential equation : 

$$
- \bigg( \frac{\partial C} {\partial t} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2}\bigg) dt =  r \bigg(-C + \frac{\partial C} {\partial S} S \bigg) dt  
$$

$$
\frac{\partial C}{\partial t} + rS \frac{\partial C}{\partial S} + \frac{1}{2} \sigma^2 S^2 \frac{\partial^2 C}{\partial S^2} -rC = 0 \tag{1.12}
$$

The above equation holds for any derivate whose pay-off depends only on $S$ and $t$. For example it holds for standard European call and put options, as well as for American options, lookback options and barrier options. 

If the maturity date of the option is given by $T$ and the strike price is given by $K$ then these boundary conditions can be expressed as 

$$
C_T = max(0,S_T - K)
$$

for a call option

$$
C_T = max(0,K -S_T)
$$

for a put option. 

*Apart from the fundamental assumption describing the asset price movements, other assumptions that we implicitly made when deriving equation (1.12) are that the short selling of securities with full use of the proceeds if permitted, there are no transaction costs, all securities are infinitely divisible, no dividents are paid during the life of the derivative security, the riskless rate of interest is constent and the same for all maturities, and that security trading is continuous.*

Perhaps surprisingly, the expected return on the asset $(\mu)$ does not appear in the Black-Scholes partial differential equation. Since the expected return on the asset does not appear in the Black-Scholes partial differential equation the value of the derivative must be independent of investor risk preferences. 

Therefore, one can assume any risk preferences in the valuation of the derivative or put another way, one can assume the drift of asset price be anything . Assuming risk neutrality , i.e investors are risk neutral, then all assets earn the riskless rate of interest, and equation (1.1) can be rewritten as 

$$
dS = rS dt + \sigma S dz \tag{1.13}
$$

If the asset pays out a continuous dividend yield at a rate $\delta$ per annum, then the drift rate is reduced by this payout and the process becomes. 

$$
dS = (r-\delta)S dt + \sigma S dz \tag{1.14}
$$

The implication of this risk-neutral pricing is that the **present value of any future random cash flow ( eg. the pay-off to an option) is given by the expected value of the random future value discounted at the riskless rate.**  This leads to the so-called expectations approach of option valuation , e.g for a European call option : 

$$
C = E_t^* [ e^{- \int_t^T r_u du} max(0,S_T - K)] \tag{1.15}
$$

where $E_t^*[.]$ is the expectation at time $t$, the valuation date, under the risk-neutral probabilities.  For constant interest rates , the above equation can be written as 

$$
C = e^{-r(T-t)} E_t^* [ max(0,S_T-K)] \tag{\hspace{0.5cm}1.16}
$$
